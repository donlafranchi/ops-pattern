# Boise, Idaho — Seed Market Research

## Thesis

Boise's massive influx from CA, OR, and WA creates a population actively asking "where do I find my people here?" These are people who *left somewhere and chose somewhere* — they need to discover their new community. This tests whether the platform helps people who lack existing local networks.

---

## 1. Farmers Market Scene

Boise has a rich, mature farmers market ecosystem — two flagship Saturday markets plus a constellation of neighborhood and suburban markets across the Treasure Valley.

**Capital City Public Market** is Idaho's largest outdoor market. Founded 1994, now in its 31st year. Runs every Saturday, April through December (roughly 36 weeks — one of the longest seasons in the Mountain West). 150+ rotating vendors, all Idaho-based. Located at 422 S 11th Street in downtown Boise (moved for 2025 season). Thousands of weekly shoppers. A mix of produce, baked goods, crafts, and specialty items. This is the crown jewel — the community anchor.

**Boise Farmers Market** operates Saturdays, April through October, 9am–1pm at 1500 Shoreline Drive (moving to the Idaho Botanical Garden in late 2026). This is the second major Saturday market, distinct from Capital City.

**Treasure Valley market network** spans the metro area with 8+ markets:

- Eagle Saturday Market — Eagle City Hall campus, Saturdays 9am–1pm
- Meridian Main Street Market — Saturdays 9am–1pm, April–October
- Meridian Monday Night Market — Storey Park, Mondays 4pm–9pm, June–September
- South Meridian Market — Wednesdays 5pm–8pm, April–October
- Nampa Farmers Market — Lloyd Square Park, Saturdays 9am–1:30pm, April–November
- Caldwell Farm to Fork Market — Indian Creek Plaza, Tuesdays 5pm–8pm

**Season length advantage:** The April–December window (Capital City) and April–October window (most others) gives 6–8 months of active market season. Long enough to establish the platform's presence over a single growing season.

**Key signal:** The market scene is *organized, established, and community-oriented* — not just transactional. These markets function as weekly gathering spaces, which is exactly the wedge the platform needs.

## 2. Local Business / Maker Ecosystem

Boise has an unusually strong independent business culture, particularly downtown.

**96% of downtown Boise businesses are locally owned.** For every $100 spent at a local business, $65 recirculates locally. This is a remarkable density of independents and suggests a cultural identity around local ownership.

**Buy Idaho** is Idaho's first shop-local network with 1,000+ member businesses statewide. Members get directory listings, print directories, and featured placement at Buy Idaho events including a holiday market and the Capitol Show.

**Idaho Preferred** is a state-run program (Idaho State Department of Agriculture, since 2002) that brands and promotes Idaho-grown, raised, and crafted products. Includes a Producer Map, Farm + Food Finder directory, and CSA Week promotions. Over 185 crops and commodities. This is state-level infrastructure for exactly the kind of discovery the platform enables.

**Artisan and maker spaces:**

- **Handmade Idaho** — artisan co-op at Boise Towne Square Mall showcasing small makers across the state. Grew from demand for more vendor opportunities.
- **Idaho Made** — premier artisan co-op supporting 60+ local creators.
- **Lark and Larder** — neighborhood market on the Boise Bench offering local/regional products from farmers, producers, artists, and makers (glass, ceramics, jewelry, textiles, health/beauty).

**Key signal:** The buy-local identity is deeply embedded — not aspirational, operational. The 96% independent downtown stat is extraordinary. The platform would reinforce an existing cultural value, not try to create one.

## 3. Community Organizations

**Neighborhood Associations:** Boise has 30+ established neighborhood associations. The North End neighborhood is especially organized (its own website at northend.org, nonprofit directory, historic preservation advocacy).

**City of Boise Office of Community Engagement** — a dedicated city department fostering citizen connections. This is institutional infrastructure for community building.

**Neighbors United Boise** — a City of Boise + Idaho Office for Refugees collaborative focused on refugee integration. Programs include Global Gardens (refugee-run CSA), Global Talent Idaho (employment), English Language Center, Refugee Speakers Bureau, Afghan Cultural Center, and HouseYourNeighbor.org. Over 100 community organization leaders, policymakers, and service providers participate.

**VillageCo** — a grassroots, Boise-based nonprofit running since August 2022. Helps people form small intentional groups called "Villages" for real connection and shared responsibility. Monthly potlucks, park gatherings. 30 volunteers organized in sociocratic circles. Running a Village Growing Cohort to help community builders start new Villages. This is *exactly* the kind of organization that would champion the platform.

**Boise Mutual Aid** — built around the idea that neighborly assistance isn't charity. Open avenues for anyone to give food, clothing, or time.

**Downtown Boise Foundation** — 501(c)(3) nonprofit building community and celebrating culture downtown. Runs multiple free programs and events annually.

**Key signal:** VillageCo is the standout — a grassroots community-building experiment explicitly solving the "how do I find my people" problem. Natural partner. The broader ecosystem (30+ neighborhood associations, city engagement office, mutual aid) shows institutional and grassroots infrastructure for community organizing.

## 4. Local Food & Farm Networks

**Boise Co-op** — community-owned natural foods market, founded 1973. Two locations (Boise North End and The Village at Meridian). Sources from 300+ local vendors. 20% discount on local vendor products twice monthly for members. $50 one-time membership. Runs a Local Partner Fund (annual grant from customer round-up donations to support local vendors). This is a 50+ year community institution with deep local producer relationships.

**CSA programs in the Treasure Valley:**

- FarmDeliver + Dry Creek Growers — 20-week CSA with home delivery across Boise, Meridian, Eagle, Hidden Springs, Kuna, Caldwell, Nampa, Star
- Global Gardens Boise — summer CSA from refugee-background farmers
- Fiddler's Green Farm, Happy Heron Farm, Lightfield Acres, Living Land Ranch, Peaceful Belly Farms, Spyglass Gardens — all active Treasure Valley CSAs
- Idaho has 80+ CSA programs statewide

**FARE Idaho** — 501(c)(6) trade association representing independent food and beverage businesses. 312+ members. Connects producers to retailers/consumers. Runs the Field to Fork festival. Advocates for policies supporting small food businesses. This is the organized voice of the local food economy.

**Farmer-Chef Collaborative** — an Idaho State Department of Agriculture program (since 2008) connecting farmers, ranchers, and food producers with restaurant chefs. Hosts networking events, produces video series. With 185+ commodities grown in Idaho, the farm-to-table pipeline is active.

**Treasure Valley Food Coalition** — 501(c)(3) promoting the local food economy across a 9-county Idaho/Oregon region.

**Key signal:** The local food network is *dense and organized* — state programs, trade associations, food coalitions, a 50-year-old co-op, dozens of CSAs. The platform's farmer/producer discovery function would plug into existing infrastructure rather than building from scratch.

## 5. Tech / Startup Scene

Boise has a legitimate and growing tech ecosystem — not San Francisco, but enough for early-adopter density.

**Major tech employers:**

- **Micron Technology** — headquartered in Boise. Building a $15B advanced-memory fab. Expected to create 17,000+ new jobs. This is a semiconductor anchor.
- **HP Inc.** — significant Boise presence (printers/PCs division)
- **Clearwater Analytics** — public fintech (IPO'd 2021), downtown Boise. Investment accounting platforms.
- **Cradlepoint** (Ericsson) — 5G WAN platforms, based in Meridian
- **Kount** — AI-powered fraud prevention, $600B+ in annual payment transactions

Tech job growth: 32.4% from 2016–2021. Boise ranks #7 nationally for tech job growth. Inc. magazine named it one of the best U.S. cities for entrepreneurs.

**Startup infrastructure:**

- **Trailhead** — startup incubator and coworking space, founded 2015 by entrepreneurs and City of Boise. Has supported $240M+ in awards and investments for members. Programs include Idaho Startup Lab (10-week sprint), Tree City Trailblazers (9-month cohort with Perkins Coie), Trail Guides (mentorship network), and Trailmix ($50K food/beverage pitch competition with Albertsons shelf space).
- **Boise Entrepreneur Week** — Idaho's largest entrepreneur event. Sept 28–Oct 2, 2026 at JUMP. Pitch competitions, youth innovation challenge, education tracks.
- **Idaho Startup Lab** — revived at Boise State in 2025. 10-week mentor-driven program.
- **Idaho SBDC** — Small Business Development Center, offices in Boise and Nampa, operating since 1986.

**Tech meetups:** AI Tinkerers Boise (40–60 builders monthly), AI Wednesdays (weekly), BSU AI Brownbag series, Start.Boise (Meetup group), Boise Remote Workers (very active — happy hours, dinners).

**JUMP (Jack's Urban Meeting Place)** — nonprofit interactive creative center and community gathering space in downtown Boise. 3-acre public park with amphitheater, studios, meeting areas. Hosts Boise Entrepreneur Week and community events year-round. This is a physical community anchor.

**Key signal:** Enough tech workers and startup culture for early-adopter density. The Micron expansion (17,000 jobs) will bring another wave of newcomers. Trailhead's Trailmix competition (food/beverage startups + Albertsons) shows tech-meets-local-food crossover that aligns perfectly with the platform.

## 6. The Newcomer Angle

This is the strongest signal for the thesis.

**Migration data:** Idaho was the #1 moving destination in the U.S. in 2025. The Boise MSA (Ada, Canyon, Boise, Gem, Owyhee counties) grew at 2.2%, making it the 13th fastest-growing market nationally and 2nd fastest in the West (behind only St. George, Utah).

**Origin states for Boise specifically (2023 Census):**
- California: 10,073 people (23% of out-of-state movers)
- Washington: 2,776 (7%)
- Oregon: 2,332 (9%)
- Utah: ~9%

Statewide, 17,000+ moved from California, 14,000+ from Washington, 7,000+ from Oregon in a single year. Nearly 28% of new residents — approximately 72,800 people — moved from out of state.

**Scale of growth:** Ada and Canyon Counties have added 121,768 people since the 2020 Census (+14%). The population has nearly doubled in 25 years (432,345 in 2000 → 847,840 in 2025). Ada County alone added 10,000+ residents in 2025.

**Newcomer resources and communities:**

- **VLLG** — a friendship-matching app based on values, interests, and location, *created in Boise*. This is a direct signal that the "find my people" problem is acute enough to spawn local startups.
- **VillageCo** — grassroots intentional community building (described above)
- **Boise Remote Workers Meetup** — very active group for work-from-home transplants
- **Boise Area Runners** — casual running group that doubles as a social connector (runs → coffee/meals)
- **AAUW Newcomers Group** — monthly social for new members
- **Facebook groups:** Boise Socialite, 208 Anglers, The Volley Gang (pickup volleyball at Ann Morrison Park)
- **From Boise** newsletter — 23,000+ subscribers, explicitly positioned as the "local's guide" with a regular "how to make friends in Boise" feature

**Key signal:** The newcomer problem is *acute and acknowledged*. A Boise-born friendship app (VLLG), a grassroots community-building nonprofit (VillageCo), and a 23K-subscriber newsletter running "how to make friends" content all point to unsatisfied demand for exactly what the platform offers. The scale is massive — 10,000+ people per year arriving in Ada County alone, predominantly from CA/OR/WA.

## 7. Local Media & Influencers

**Digital-first local media:**

- **BoiseDev** — daily newsletter covering Boise business, development, and growth. The go-to source for what's happening in the local economy.
- **From Boise** — 23,000+ subscriber newsletter. Local events, restaurants, community features. Positioned as the insider's guide.
- **Totally Boise** — hyperlocal marketing platform with business directory, women-owned business directory, and community content (local stories, artisan market coverage). Office at 999 W Main St.
- **This Is Boise** — community organization running Small Business Saturday (75+ local vendors), annual event calendar, and local business features.
- **City Cast Boise** — local podcast/newsletter brand (part of City Cast network)
- **Boise Lifestyle Magazine** — monthly print + digital publication spotlighting families, businesses, charities, restaurants

**Food-specific media:**

- **Boifood Media** (Alex) — Boise's top food influencer. Restaurant features, food events, chef stories.
- **@treasurevalley_treatsandeats** (Lauren) — 37K followers. Coffee shops, restaurants, bars, local businesses.
- **@Boise_BucketList** (Diana DeJesus) — 30,000+ followers. Created when she moved to Boise to explore the area — she *is* the newcomer discovering the city.

**Traditional media:** KTVB (NBC), KBOI (CBS), Idaho News 6 (ABC/CW), Idaho Press, Boise State Public Radio (NPR affiliate). Six TV stations, two dailies, three weeklies, 19 commercial radio stations.

**Key signal:** @Boise_BucketList is the archetype of the platform's user — a transplant who started documenting local discovery and built a 30K audience doing it. The local media ecosystem is active and local-business-friendly. BoiseDev and From Boise are high-signal channels for reaching the exact audience the platform needs.

## 8. Growth Dynamics

**Current metro population:** Boise city proper: 253,550 (2025). Boise MSA: ~482,000. Ada + Canyon Counties: 847,840.

**Growth rate:** Boise MSA growing at 2.2% annually (13th fastest in the U.S., 2nd in the West). Ada County added 10,000+ residents in 2025 alone.

**Doubling:** Ada + Canyon Counties had 432,345 people in 2000 and 847,840 in 2025 — nearly doubled in 25 years.

**Where the growth is happening:**

- **Meridian** — Boise's largest suburb, massive development. The District at 10 Mile is a $1B mixed-use development (4x the size of The Village at Meridian). Basque Ranch: 570 home sites on 470 acres in north Meridian. Emblem Meridian: 250-unit development at Eagle Rd/Victory Rd.
- **Star** — small-town charm + rapid new development. A blend of rural character and suburban expansion.
- **Eagle** — upscale suburban growth northwest of Boise
- **Nampa/Caldwell** — more affordable western Treasure Valley growth
- **Southeast Boise** — city planning major development near new Micron fab

**New neighborhood formation:** These suburban expansion areas (Star, south Meridian, southeast Boise) are *exactly* where newcomers land and where existing community networks are thinnest. New subdivisions with no established gathering places = maximum demand for the platform's discovery function.

**Key signal:** Growth is fastest in the suburban ring (Meridian, Star, Eagle) where community infrastructure hasn't caught up with housing. The Micron expansion will accelerate this. New neighborhoods full of transplants with no local ties = the platform's ideal conditions.

## 9. Existing Competition / Similar Tools

**Nextdoor** — present in Boise (as everywhere). Neighborhood-scoped, safety/complaint-oriented. Not a discovery or mutual-support platform. Does not serve the "find my people" need.

**Facebook Groups** — the default. Multiple Boise-specific groups (Boise Socialite, neighborhood groups, interest groups). Fragmented, no structure, algorithmic feed. High friction for discovery.

**Meetup** — active in Boise (board games, running, AI, remote workers). Interest-locked and event-driven. Doesn't connect producers to consumers or organize around place.

**VLLG** — Boise-born friendship app matching on values/interests/location. Direct signal of the problem, but narrow (1-to-1 matching, not community-wide discovery).

**VillageCo** — intentional small-group formation. Complementary, not competitive. Too small-scale and manual to serve as a discovery platform.

**Buy Idaho** / **Idaho Preferred** — directories, not platforms. Static listings, no community interaction, no real-time discovery.

**Totally Boise** / **This Is Boise** — local business directories + event calendars. Marketing-oriented, not community-oriented. No user-generated content, no mutual support.

**From Boise** — newsletter, not a platform. One-directional. Can't replace what the platform offers but is a strong distribution channel.

**Key signal:** No one owns the integrated local-discovery + mutual-support space. The closest competitors are either too narrow (VLLG = friendship only), too generic (Nextdoor = complaints), too fragmented (Facebook = algorithmic chaos), or too static (directories = no community). The gap is real.

## 10. Champion Profiles

Based on all of the above, these are the 5 champion types most likely to adopt early, bring their networks, and evangelize:

### Champion 1: The Market Manager / Organizer

**Who:** The person running Capital City Public Market, Boise Farmers Market, or any of the Treasure Valley markets. Also: FARE Idaho leadership, Treasure Valley Food Coalition organizers.

**Why they champion:** They already coordinate 150+ vendors and thousands of consumers weekly. They know every producer personally. They suffer the pain of vendor communication, consumer discovery, and seasonal engagement. The platform gives their community a year-round digital home.

**What they bring:** Immediate vendor network (150+ producers), consumer foot traffic, credibility with the local food ecosystem.

### Champion 2: The Transplant Community Builder

**Who:** The person who moved from Portland/Seattle/Bay Area 1–3 years ago and has become the "connector" — the one who organizes dinners, creates group chats, starts the neighborhood potluck. VillageCo volunteers. VLLG early adopters. @Boise_BucketList-type content creators. The person running the "New to Boise" Facebook group.

**Why they champion:** They lived the pain. They spent 6–12 months feeling isolated and built their own informal discovery system. They want what the platform offers and will tell every new arrival about it.

**What they bring:** Social proof with other newcomers, content creation instinct, willingness to evangelize because they remember the pain.

### Champion 3: The Local Maker / Artisan Producer

**Who:** The person selling at Handmade Idaho, Idaho Made, or Lark and Larder. The CSA farmer offering subscriptions through FarmDeliver. The food crafter in Idaho Preferred. The soap maker at Capital City Public Market.

**Why they champion:** They need customers but hate Instagram marketing. They sell at 2–3 markets per week and do CSA delivery but have no single place where their fans can find them, follow them, and know where they'll be next Saturday. The platform is their owned channel.

**What they bring:** Product inventory, market presence, authentic stories, existing customer base they'll invite onto the platform.

### Champion 4: The Tech-Adjacent Entrepreneur

**Who:** Trailhead members. Boise Entrepreneur Week participants. Trailmix food/beverage competitors. Clearwater Analytics or Micron employees who also run a side business (hobby farm, weekend market booth, community project). Remote workers from the Boise Remote Workers meetup.

**Why they champion:** They understand platforms, they appreciate the mission, and many of them have a dual identity — tech professional + local community participant. They're early adopters by temperament and will give product feedback, not just use it.

**What they bring:** Product sense, willingness to tolerate early-stage roughness, social networks that span both the tech and local-community worlds.

### Champion 5: The Neighborhood Association / Civic Leader

**Who:** Leaders of Boise's 30+ neighborhood associations (especially in fast-growing areas: south Meridian, Star, southeast Boise). City of Boise Office of Community Engagement contacts. Downtown Boise Foundation organizers.

**Why they champion:** They're trying to build community in neighborhoods that didn't exist 3 years ago. Their tools are email lists and Facebook pages. They need a way to help new residents discover what's around them — the farmers market two miles away, the neighbor who sells eggs, the monthly potluck in the park.

**What they bring:** Geographic authority, institutional credibility, direct connection to the people who most need the platform (new residents in new neighborhoods).

## Market Assessment Summary

| Factor | Signal Strength | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Farmers market density & maturity | **Strong** | 8+ markets, 36-week season, 150+ vendors at flagship |
| Independent business culture | **Very strong** | 96% locally owned downtown, Buy Idaho (1,000+ members), state-level Idaho Preferred program |
| Community org infrastructure | **Strong** | 30+ neighborhood associations, city engagement office, VillageCo, mutual aid |
| Local food network | **Very strong** | Boise Co-op (50 years, 300+ local vendors), 80+ CSAs statewide, FARE Idaho (312+ members), Farmer-Chef Collaborative |
| Tech / early-adopter density | **Moderate-strong** | Micron, Clearwater, Trailhead, AI meetups. Not SF but sufficient. Growing fast. |
| Newcomer pain point | **Very strong** | #1 moving destination in U.S., 10K+ arrivals/year in Ada County, 23% from CA. VLLG and VillageCo exist because the pain is acute. |
| Local media friendliness | **Strong** | BoiseDev, From Boise (23K subs), Totally Boise, food influencers. All local-business-friendly. |
| Growth dynamics | **Very strong** | 2.2% annual metro growth, doubling in 25 years, new neighborhoods forming in real time |
| Competitive gap | **Very strong** | No integrated local-discovery + mutual-support platform exists. Fragmented across Nextdoor, Facebook, directories, newsletters. |
| Champion accessibility | **Strong** | Market managers, community builders, makers, and tech entrepreneurs all reachable through identifiable organizations |

**Verdict:** Boise is a high-signal seed market. The newcomer thesis is supported by hard data (10K+/year from CA/OR/WA), validated by local startups solving adjacent problems (VLLG, VillageCo), and reinforced by an unusually strong local-business and local-food ecosystem. The competitive gap is real — no one owns this space. The farmers market wedge has ideal conditions: multiple large markets, long season, organized management, strong vendor networks. The main risk is that Boise's growth could slow (it's already moderating from pandemic peaks), but the installed base of recent transplants who haven't yet found their community is large enough to sustain initial traction regardless.
