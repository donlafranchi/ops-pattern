# Savannah, Georgia — Seed Market Research

**For: Movers, Makers & Shakers launch decision**
**Date: June 22, 2026**

---

## Executive Summary

Savannah is a strong seed market candidate. The thesis holds: tight-knit community, active local food movement, maker/artisan culture fueled by SCAD, and a tourist economy that has pushed residents to actively self-organize around local identity. The critical finding is that **there is zero modern platform infrastructure** connecting the community institutions that already exist. Farmers markets run on Instagram and basic websites. Buy Local Savannah has 150+ member businesses but only a WordPress directory. Event discovery is fragmented across 10+ channels. No resident-first digital platform exists — everything is either tourist-facing or stuck in the newsletter/directory era. The gap between community energy and community tooling is enormous.

Five champion types emerge: market managers (starting with Forsyth Farmers' Market), shop-local organizers (Buy Local Savannah, Make Savannah), neighborhood association leaders (especially Downtown Neighborhood Association), SCAD-connected maker-entrepreneurs, and local food system advocates (Healthy Savannah, Food Policy Council).

## 1. Farmers Market Scene

### Market Inventory

Savannah metro has **6 distinct farmers/community markets**:

**Forsyth Farmers' Market** — The flagship. South end of Forsyth Park, every Saturday 9am–1pm, year-round, rain or shine. Founded 2009 by six women as a 501(c)(3) nonprofit. **60–70+ vendors** on any given Saturday, all producing at least 75% of what they sell, sourced within 150–200 miles of Savannah. Producer-only, food-and-plant-only — no resellers, no crafts. This is the community's weekly town square. No published attendance numbers exist, but it's consistently ranked #1 by every local and tourism source.

Forsyth runs substantial programs beyond the Saturday market: **Farm Truck 912** (Georgia's first mobile farmers market, delivering SNAP-eligible produce to 12 underserved neighborhoods weekly), **912 Food Farmacy**, **Forsyth Family Farm Share** (CSA-style boxes for childhood food insecurity, co-launched with Georgia Organics), a Youth Booth, and SNAP/EBT dollar-for-dollar doubling via Wholesome Wave Georgia. In January 2025, added nine new vendors. In February 2025, opened a **Corner Store** at 6228 Waters Avenue (Tue–Fri 10am–2pm). Also launched biweekly Fresh Perspectives workshops (gardening, composting, mushroom cultivation).

**Savannah State Farmers Market** — 701 US Highway 80 West. Year-round, 8am–4pm. Georgia Department of Agriculture (one of nine statewide). Wholesale and retail since 1953. Seasonal produce, nursery plants, on-site restaurant.

**Farmers & Fishermen Sunset Market** — Thursday evenings, 4:30–8:30pm. Alternates between Savannah Harbor (1st/3rd/5th Thursdays) and The Park at Eastern Wharf (2nd/4th). Local farmers, fishermen, food artisans, small-batch makers. Live music, yoga, family/dog-friendly.

**Islands Farmers' & Community Market** — Saturdays 9am–noon on the road to Tybee Island. Seasonal (late February through mid-June). Fresh produce, local meat, honey, artisans.

**Tybee Island Farmers & Artisans Market** — Behind the Historic Tybee Lighthouse. Weekly with produce, baked goods, honey, artisans.

**Richmond Hill Farmers Market** — JF Gregory Park Pavilion, 30 min south. Seasonal May–November. First Saturday of each month is a dedicated "Makers Market."

### Season Length

Year-round operation is confirmed for Forsyth and the State Market. The Sunset Market runs on a regular Thursday schedule. Savannah's mild climate supports longer seasons than northern states — most seasonal markets run February through November or later.

### Technology

**No dedicated market app, no online ordering, no vendor-to-customer messaging, no pre-order system, no loyalty features, no SNAP-tracking tools.** Forsyth communicates via Instagram (23K followers), Facebook, and a basic Squarespace website. Vendor applications are handled through the website. The entire technology stack is: social media + basic website + physical token system for SNAP. No evidence of WhatsGood, Harvie, FarmFan, or any market-specific platform being used anywhere in Savannah.

## 2. Local Business & Maker Ecosystem

### Business Density and Associations

**Savannah Area Chamber of Commerce** — 2,300+ members. Partners with City on economic development.

**Savannah Downtown Business Association (SDBA)** — ~200 members (retailers, restaurateurs, professional services). Founded 1997 by and for downtown business owners. Julie Wade named Board President for 2025–2027.

**Buy Local Savannah** — 150+ locally-owned member businesses in Chatham County. Mission: support independent businesses, prevent displacement by national/global chains. 2024 President: Somi Benson-Jaja. Runs the annual **Shop 912 Weekend** (September 12–14, tied to the 912 area code) with the Chamber. Pushes the "$68 of every $100 stays local" message. **27 years old.** But the technology is a WordPress site with a member directory — no marketplace, no consumer-facing discovery, no e-commerce.

**Make Savannah** — Maker collective. Curated small business directory (~50+ listings), individual artisan "Makers" directory, "Shop Savannah Made" online shop, events, newsletter. Squarespace site. Strong SCAD community connection. More artisan/maker focused than general economy.

### Key Independent Business Clusters

**Broughton Street** — Premier shopping street. Leopold's Ice Cream (est. 1919), Savannah Bee Company, E. Shaver Booksellers, 24E Design Co., The Grey (modern Southern cuisine in restored Greyhound station).

**City Market** — Built 1755. 40+ merchants including Little Crown by Pie Society, Jery's Baskets (Smithsonian collection), Savannah's Candy Kitchen.

**Starland District** — ~35 blocks south of Forsyth Park. This is the maker/creative heart. **Founded in 1999 by two SCAD historic preservation graduates** (John Deaderick and Greg Jacobs) who purchased the former Starland Dairy factory to create an art and design district. Notable: Foxy Loxy Cafe, Graveface Records, Starlandia Art Supply. **Starland Village** is a newer mixed-use development with 16,000+ sq ft creative office, 10+ professional artist studios, plus food/shopping/wellness.

### Maker/Artisan Infrastructure

**Bull Street Labs** — 6,000 sq ft coworking at 2222 Bull Street in Starland. Largest coworking space in Coastal Georgia. Operated by The Creative Coast. Coworking, events, classes.

**Switchyards** — Opening a 24/7 coworking space in Starland. $100/month. First Georgia club outside Atlanta.

**Arts Southeast** — Founded 2014. Houses 26 private artist studios, the Ellis Gallery, the On::View Artist Residency. Hosts the monthly **First Friday Art March**.

**Dreamhouse Studios** — Female-led artist workspace, seven resident artists, monthly workshops (quilting, ceramics, watercolor).

**Knack Studio** — Pottery and creative experiences. Wheel-throwing classes, handmade ceramics.

### Markets and Art Events

**First Friday Art March** — Monthly since 2012. Every first Friday 5–9pm. Free trolley to galleries around and south of Forsyth Park. Run by Art Rise Savannah (nonprofit).

**SLAM (Savannah Local Artist Market)** — Annual, 75+ local/regional artists.

**Crafts on the River** — Multiple events per season at Rousakis Riverfront Plaza, up to 70 artists/crafters.

**Gordonston Art Fair** — Annual juried fine arts/crafts fair.

### Startup/Incubator Ecosystem

**The Creative Coast** — Savannah's leading nonprofit for innovation/entrepreneurship. Runs **SPARK Savannah** (12-week accelerator, launched March 2025, 15 entrepreneurs, no cost, at SCAD's Deloitte Foundry), annual Idea Accelerator Bootcamp, Bridge Fund (GA/SC early-stage funding).

**ATDC Savannah** — Georgia Tech's Advanced Technology Development Center. Statewide network; 170+ startups, $3B raised, $12.5B sales historically.

**Savannah Entrepreneurial Center** — City-run. Mentoring, technical assistance, training, networking.

## 3. SCAD Connection

### Economic Impact

SCAD generated **$1.3 billion** in annual economic impact on Georgia in FY2023 (70% increase from FY2019). **$1 billion** of that in the Savannah area specifically. Supports **12,256 jobs** statewide. $38.5M annually in charitable donations and volunteer services.

### Alumni Retention — The Maker Pipeline

**39% of SCAD alumni remain in Georgia** after graduating (~17,940 living/working in state from 46,000 total alumni). Alumni have established **233+ businesses in Savannah** specifically. 99% of Spring 2022 graduates were employed or pursuing further education within 10 months.

The Starland District itself — Savannah's creative/maker hub — was founded by SCAD graduates.

### SCAD-Run Commerce

**shopSCAD** — Retail boutique at 340 Bull Street. Exclusively artwork and designs by SCAD students, professors, staff, and alumni. Fine art, jewelry, clothing, handbags, home goods.

**BAZAAR by shopSCAD** — Luxury boutique at 318 E. Liberty St. Atelier-created ready-to-wear, accessories, jewelry, ceramics, objets d'art. Positions student/alumni work in a luxury retail framework.

**SCAD Museum of Art** — Contemporary art museum. Rotating quarterly exhibitions. Past shows: Oscar de la Renta, Vivienne Westwood, Carrie Mae Weems.

### Entrepreneurship Programs

**SCADpro** — University design/innovation studio at the Deloitte Foundry. Connects students with Fortune 500 clients. Launches alumni businesses. Provides executive education.

**SCADpro Fund** — Invests **$100,000 in exchange for equity** in early-stage SCAD alumni entrepreneurs, with mentoring and innovation support.

**Alumni Atelier** — Ambassadorship program for graduates pursuing creative practice + entrepreneurship. Provides time, space, resources, and business education.

**Creative Business Leadership Program** — Merges entrepreneurship with design knowledge (strategy, financial planning, management).

## 4. Community Organizations

### Neighborhood System

Savannah's Oglethorpe Plan (1733) organized the city into wards around **24 central squares**. This physical structure creates inherent neighborhood identity — residents orient by squares and wards, not just addresses. The City maintains an **Open Neighborhoods** mapping application (SAGIS) tracking dozens of registered associations.

Named active associations include: Downtown Neighborhood Association (DNA — 501(c)(3), founded 1974, the most prominent), Thomas Square Neighborhood Association (founded 2015, bi-monthly meetings, monthly litter cleanups), Victorian Neighborhood Association, Ardsley Park/Chatham Crescent Neighborhood Association, Gordonston, Live Oak, Cuyler-Brownville, West Savannah, and many others.

No single umbrella federation exists. The closest coordinating structures are the City's **Office of Neighborhood Safety and Engagement (ONSE)** (grants up to $15,000 to qualified associations, Neighborhood Improvement Teams) and **Step Up Savannah's Neighborhood Leadership Academy** (12-week leadership development for emerging community leaders).

### Key Civic Organizations

**Savannah Community Foundation** — Founded 1956. Grants **$7.25M+ annually** to 250+ organizations. Part of a landscape of 188 foundations in the metro area with combined assets of $395M.

**Step Up Savannah** — Poverty reduction initiative. Runs six direct service programs including the Neighborhood Leadership Academy and **Savannah Votes** (civic engagement, launched 2018). Instrumental in establishing the Savannah Affordable Housing Fund ($500K/year from City as of 2021).

**Deep Center** — Youth literacy and community storytelling nonprofit, founded 2008. Works with 700+ youth and 200+ adults annually. Signature: **Block by Block** (high schoolers document neighborhood histories). Funded by NEA, Kresge Foundation.

**Junior League of Savannah** — 90+ years of continuous operation.

**Historic Savannah Foundation (HSF)** — Founded 1955. Has directly saved 350+ buildings. Operates the **Green Pages** — a directory of businesses supporting preservation, creating a preservation-commerce alliance. No explicit chain store ban exists, but the historic overlay districts' design review processes and physical constraints (small storefronts, restricted signage) functionally favor independents over cookie-cutter chains.

**Bike Walk Savannah** — Advocacy for pedestrian/cycling infrastructure. Connected to the Tide to Town urban trail system (30-mile network) and Coastal Georgia Greenway (150 miles).

### Community Development — The Equity Dimension

**Savannah Community Land Trust** — Incorporated 2020. Long-term affordable housing, anti-gentrification, financial education, entrepreneurship fostering. Starting in West Savannah.

**Community Housing Services Agency** — HUD-certified. Has built nine homes on Cumming Street in West Savannah.

Key context: **~45% of Chatham County residents** live more than one mile from the nearest grocery store. **17.6%** food insecure overall; **21.8%** of children food insecure. Majority-Black neighborhoods closer to fast food than supermarkets. This explains the heavy food-access emphasis in the local food ecosystem.

## 5. Local Food & Farm Networks

### Farm-to-Table

Well-developed scene. Key restaurants: **Cha Bella** (pioneer of Savannah's farm-to-table movement, nearly entire menu sourced locally), **Husk Savannah** (Sean Brock's strict Southern sourcing), **Common Thread** (Lowcountry ingredients, global approaches), **Local 11 Ten** (seasonal menus), **The Green Truck Pub** (grass-fed from Hunter Cattle Company in nearby Brooklet, GA). Robb Report has profiled Savannah as "one of the South's most exciting dining destinations."

### CSA Programs

**Junie B Farms CSA** — Year-round, flexible membership, delivers to Savannah area.
**Forsyth Family Farm Share** — Group-style CSA through Forsyth Farmers' Market + Georgia Organics. Targeted at childhood food insecurity.
**Local Farmbag** — Home delivery of local produce, started when a downtown market went on hiatus. Year-round.
**Farmers Fresh CSA** — Four-season CSA + online market.

### No Formal Food Co-op

**Brighter Day Natural Foods** (since 1978, 1102 Bull Street) is the closest analog — independent, locally-owned natural grocery — but it's privately owned, not a cooperative.

### Food System Organizations

**Savannah Chatham Food Policy Council** — Restructured recently by Healthy Savannah. Three top priorities for 2025 around equitable food access.

**Healthy Savannah** — "Making a healthy choice the easy choice." Received a **CDC REACH grant of $5.1M** (five-year, awarded September 2023) partnered with YMCA. Runs H.O.P.E. project targeting health disparities in seven low-wealth zip codes. Instrumental in Savannah's "Georgia Food Oasis" designation.

**Georgia Organics** — Statewide nonprofit with significant Savannah programming. Co-launched Family Farm Share, runs Farm to School in Chatham County schools.

**Wholesome Wave Georgia** — SNAP-doubling "Fresh for Less" program. Forsyth Farmers' Market and Farm Truck 912 are among biggest partners. 80% of shoppers increased daily fruit/vegetable servings.

**Second Harvest of Coastal Georgia** — Food bank serving 21 coastal counties. 40+ years. 147,000+ people, 245+ nonprofit partners, 85 Kids Cafes serving 4,200+ hot meals daily.

### Urban Agriculture

**CJ's Produce** — Medley of backyard and community gardens producing for majority-Black neighborhoods with low food access. Weekend demos.
**Brickyard Gardens** — Ag nonprofit founded 2024.
**Fruitful Futures** — Ag nonprofit founded 2023.
**SCAD Back 40 Farm** — Donates produce to Second Harvest.
**Savannah Urban Garden Alliance (SUGA)** — Referenced in Healthy Savannah programming.

## 6. Tourist vs. Local Tension

### The Numbers

**12.9 million visitors in 2024** (7.2M overnight), generating $4.1 billion in spending. 89% leisure, 68% repeat visitors. 2.9-night average stay. On a base population of ~150,000 city / ~438,000 metro — roughly 86 visitors per resident per year.

### The Friction

**Short-term rentals are the hottest issue.** 2,857 Airbnb listings with **0% holding short-term rental licenses**. City adopted ward-based caps. In late 2017, filed lawsuits against 26 illegal STVRs in Ardsley Park. **Four major neighborhood associations** (Downtown, Victorian, Thomas Square, Ardsley Park/Chatham Crescent) jointly wrote state legislators opposing HB 579 (which would strip local STVR control), calling it "the gutting of our neighborhoods." In April 2026, Chatham County held a packed public meeting on enforcement.

**Hotels are the other pressure point.** National Park Service designated the Historic Landmark District as "threatened" — WTOC found **57 hotels and inns within roughly one square mile**. Downtown Neighborhood Association: "way too many hotels."

Savannah's open-container laws make it a magnet for bachelorette/bachelor party tourism, driving noise/parking/nuisance complaints that fuel the STVR fight.

### The Geography

| Tourist zones | Local neighborhoods |
|---|---|
| River Street (universally considered tourist trap by locals) | Starland District (indie boutiques, SCAD grads, First Fridays) |
| City Market, Broughton Street shops | Ardsley Park (1910 suburb, family neighborhood) |
| Plant Riverside District (mega mixed-use resort) | Thomas Square / Streetcar District |
| Historic District squares (heavy tour groups) | Thunderbolt (shrimpers still haul catch) |
| | Wilmington Island, Isle of Hope |

### Resident Self-Organization

This community actively fights for its character. The four-association joint letter to the legislature, the packed county meetings, the Downtown Neighborhood Association's public pushback on hotels — this is not passive complaint. There is organized appetite for tools that serve residents, not tourists.

## 7. Local Media & Influencers

### Publications

**Connect Savannah** — The alt-weekly. News, music, arts, events, restaurants. Runs the annual **Best of Savannah** reader poll (currently running 2026 edition). The local-voice publication. Community editor Jessica Leigh Lebos has covered local issues for 20+ years. Also runs **SAV HAPPS** (mobile app for local events — basically a mobile frontend for their event calendar).

**Savannah Morning News** — Daily (Gannett-owned). Founded 1850. Online at SavannahNow.com. Primary daily but corporate-owned.

**The Savannah Tribune** — Weekly, founded 1875. One of the oldest African-American-owned publications in America.

**Savannah Magazine** — Lifestyle magazine. Runs a separate annual Best of Savannah awards. "The Authentic Savannah."

**Savannah Business Journal** — Business weekly, 22+ years.

**The Savannahian** — Independent digital. "The only destination for completely independent journalism, arts, and culture in Savannah."

### Key Influencers

**@eatitandlikeit (Jesse Blanco)** — **The** food authority. ~13K Instagram, Emmy-nominated show, annual "Best of Savannah" food lists. Connect Savannah's "Food Guy."

**@savannah_foodie** — Foodie's Guide to Savannah. Story highlights by restaurant type.

**@savannahfoodphotos (Savannah Eats)** — ~8,400 followers. Restaurant exploration.

**@samanthainsavannah** — ~9K. Mom + Savannah lifestyle.

**@hey_itslori** — 17.5K IG, 11.2K TikTok. Motherhood, mental health, lifestyle.

**@lowcountry_wanderer** — 20K+ TikTok. Couple-run, local destinations/restaurants/architecture.

**Key insight: The influencer landscape is mid-scale and approachable.** The biggest local food account has ~13K followers. Most are 3K–9K. No dominant gatekeeper — the space is fragmented and accessible for partnership.

### Two "Best of Savannah" Programs

These are the local tastemaker institutions:
1. **Connect Savannah's Best of Savannah** — reader poll, alt-weekly credibility
2. **Savannah Magazine's Best of Savannah** — lifestyle magazine, broader reach

## 8. Existing Competition & Discovery Tools

### Tourist-Facing (not competition)

**Visit Savannah** — The CVB. 100% tourist-focused. Hotels, restaurants, museums, tours. Does not serve residents.

### Resident-Adjacent Tools

**Savannah Master Calendar** — The closest thing to a resident-facing platform. Run by Marianne Poppell (2-person operation). Free master calendar for nonprofits/community orgs. **Weekly newsletter to 30,000+ locals.** Daily social media event coverage. Also event planning and cause marketing. But it is a newsletter + calendar — one-directional, no marketplace, no community interaction. A media company, not a platform.

**SAV HAPPS** — Connect Savannah's mobile app for local events. Bookmark events, invite friends. But it's just a mobile frontend for the newspaper's event listings.

**Connect Savannah website** — Event listings, community event submission. A newspaper website, not a platform.

**Savannah.com** — Event calendar, business listings. Tourist-skewing but some resident use.

**Buy Local Savannah** — WordPress directory. 150+ businesses. No consumer tools.

**Make Savannah** — Squarespace site. Curated directory. No marketplace functionality.

**Live Like Locals Savannah / Savannah Marketplace** — Small online marketplace for local branded goods (t-shirts, mugs). Not a community platform.

### How Residents Actually Find Things

In likely order of reach:
1. Facebook Events & Groups (dominant)
2. Savannah Master Calendar newsletter (30K+ subscribers)
3. Connect Savannah (website + SAV HAPPS)
4. Eventbrite (ticketed events)
5. Instagram (individual venue accounts)
6. Word of mouth / Nextdoor
7. Savannah.com
8. WTOC community events

**There is no Do512-style dedicated local platform for Savannah.** No city-specific event discovery with modern UX, filtering, personalization, or community features.

### The Gap

Eight structural gaps map directly to what Movers, Makers & Shakers would provide:

1. **No unified local discovery.** Events, businesses, makers, markets, groups, activities are scattered across 10+ channels.
2. **No resident-first digital platform.** Everything is tourist-facing or business-networking-facing.
3. **No local marketplace with community features.** Directories exist but with no reviews, messaging, or discovery beyond static lists.
4. **No farmer market technology.** Forsyth (60+ vendors, major anchor) runs on Instagram and tokens. Zero pre-ordering, vendor profiles, market-day communication, loyalty, or SNAP tools.
5. **No place-based community coordination.** No platform where neighbors discover what's around them, connect with local makers/sellers, or coordinate mutual support.
6. **No bridge between buy-local advocacy and actual tools.** Buy Local Savannah has been advocating for 27 years; their tech is a WordPress member list.
7. **No group/collective coordination tools.** Make Savannah's collective, mutual aid groups, neighborhood associations — all coordinate via Facebook or email.
8. **SCAD creative economy under-served.** 233+ alumni businesses in Savannah, 39% retention in Georgia, but beyond shopSCAD (one store), no platform connects makers to the community.

## 9. Champion Profiles

Based on everything above, five champion types, in priority order:

### Champion 1: Farmers Market Manager/Organizer

**Why first:** The market is the entry wedge, and Forsyth Farmers' Market is the single strongest community institution in Savannah. It's a 501(c)(3) nonprofit with 60–70 vendors, year-round operation, deep equity programming (Farm Truck 912, SNAP doubling, Family Farm Share), and zero modern tooling. The gap between what they do and what technology helps them do is massive.

**What they need:** Vendor discovery for consumers, pre-ordering, SNAP/EBT tracking, market-day communication, seasonal produce calendars, vendor profiles, community engagement beyond Instagram. Also: tools for their Farm Truck 912 mobile market and Corner Store.

**Where to find them:** Forsyth Farmers' Market nonprofit board and staff. The Sunset Market organizers. Richmond Hill's Makers Market coordinator.

### Champion 2: Shop-Local Organization Leader

**Why second:** Buy Local Savannah (150+ businesses, 27 years, named leader Somi Benson-Jaja) and Make Savannah already have the network and the message. They lack the platform. They literally exist to do what MMS does — connect people to local businesses — but their tools are WordPress directories and Squarespace pages.

**What they need:** A real discovery/marketplace layer on top of their existing networks. Member business profiles that consumers actually use. Event/market coordination tools. Data on "how much economic activity stays local" that isn't theoretical.

**Where to find them:** Buy Local Savannah (Somi Benson-Jaja, president). Make Savannah founders. SDBA (200 members, Julie Wade as board president). The Creative Coast / Bull Street Labs operators.

### Champion 3: Neighborhood Association Leader

**Why third:** The STVR fight, the hotel pushback, the four-association joint letter — neighborhood leaders are already organized, already fighting for local character, and already frustrated that existing digital tools serve tourists, not residents. They want their neighborhoods to be visible to each other, not to visitors.

**What they need:** Place-based discovery (what's in my neighborhood), group coordination tools, event/gathering visibility for residents, a way to surface local businesses/makers/services within walking distance.

**Where to find them:** Downtown Neighborhood Association (the most established, vocally opposed to over-tourism). Thomas Square Neighborhood Association (active, community-oriented, monthly cleanups). Ardsley Park/Chatham Crescent (fought HB 579). Step Up Savannah's Neighborhood Leadership Academy alumni (12-week program graduates — emerging leaders already trained in community organizing).

### Champion 4: SCAD-Connected Maker-Entrepreneur

**Why fourth:** 39% alumni retention in Georgia, 233+ alumni businesses in Savannah, SCADpro Fund investing $100K in alumni ventures, the Starland District literally founded by SCAD grads. This is a pipeline of creative people making things who need to be discovered by the local community. shopSCAD is one store. The platform gives the other 233+ businesses visibility.

**What they need:** Discovery by local buyers (not tourists), a way to signal "I'm a local maker, not a tourist shop," group coordination for maker collectives, event listings for studio tours/art walks/markets, profiles that show their work and where to find them.

**Where to find them:** shopSCAD and BAZAAR managers. SCADpro staff. Alumni Atelier participants. Arts Southeast resident artists. First Friday Art March organizers (Art Rise Savannah). Starland District business owners.

### Champion 5: Local Food System Advocate

**Why fifth:** Healthy Savannah ($5.1M CDC grant), the Food Policy Council, Georgia Organics, Wholesome Wave Georgia, the urban farming organizations — these people care deeply about connecting producers to consumers, especially in underserved neighborhoods. They have institutional credibility and grant funding. The platform's food-access layer (Farm Truck 912 coordination, SNAP tracking, produce availability by neighborhood) directly serves their mission.

**What they need:** Tools that connect food producers to food-insecure neighborhoods, SNAP/EBT program coordination, community garden visibility, CSA/farm-share discovery, food-access mapping that complements their programming.

**Where to find them:** Healthy Savannah (likely the most strategic institutional partner — they have CDC funding and cross-cutting community relationships). Forsyth Farmers' Market's equity programming staff. Georgia Organics' Savannah contacts. CJ's Produce (grassroots urban farming in majority-Black neighborhoods). Second Harvest of Coastal Georgia.

## 11. Risk Factors

**Tourist noise.** Any local discovery platform in Savannah risks being co-opted by tourist interests. The platform's resident-first positioning would need to be sharp and visible — locals are allergic to anything that feels like Visit Savannah.

**Gentrification sensitivity.** West Savannah and historically Black neighborhoods are under gentrification pressure. A platform that primarily serves the historic district / Starland / Ardsley Park could be perceived as accelerating displacement. The food-access / equity programming angle (Farm Truck 912, urban gardens, underserved neighborhoods) is essential for credibility and impact.

**Facebook entrenchment.** Neighborhood associations, market vendors, and community groups coordinate heavily through Facebook Groups. Migration to a new platform faces the cold-start problem. The wedge (farmers market) addresses this — market vendors have a transactional reason to be on the platform, not just a social one.

**Small-city economics.** 150K city / 438K metro is small. The total addressable local business market is finite. The platform needs to prove density-of-value quickly — better to go deep in one or two neighborhoods (Starland + Forsyth Park corridor) than thin across the whole metro.

**SCAD calendar.** ~14,000 students leave for summer break. The SCAD-connected maker economy has seasonal fluctuation, though alumni businesses are year-round.
