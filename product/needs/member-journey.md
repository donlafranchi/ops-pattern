**Status:** Foundational north star. Defines the loops the platform is built around, in priority order. Upstream of system specs (`member.md`, `item.md`, `location.md`, `groups.md`, etc.); downstream of the Slow Economy thesis.

## What this platform is

The platform exists so ordinary people can take ownership of their economic and civic future together — by finding each other, organizing, building, and keeping what they build. The diagnosis is the squeeze: rents rise, wages stagnate, ownership concentrates, and the institutions that once let regular people build a life have been hollowed out by extractive intermediaries. The prescription is collective action: people in a place stepping forward, showing up for each other, and gradually converting that participation into real economic stake — local businesses owned by neighbors, shared resources stewarded together, capital pooled where it's needed, and decisions made by the people they affect.

The platform is the missing coordination layer for that work. It is **not** an online third place — it does not try to be where community happens. It is the tool that makes real-world third places findable, recurring, and economically consequential. Strava, Instagram, and WhatsApp let groups organize among themselves; none of them let a stranger walking past on a Thursday find the running club at the bar. That gap, repeated across every form of organizing — gathering, sharing, trading, pooling — is what this platform fills.

## What this platform is not

This platform is **not designed to grow like Facebook**. It does not need to absorb every form of civic and economic life into a single product. Its trajectory is **outward, not inward**: as communities mature, the deeper specialized infrastructure they need — banking, insurance, education, business intelligence, payroll services, shared legal templates — should spawn into separate, federated, dedicated platforms. Mondragon is the model. Mondragon Corporation is not a single platform; it is a federation of cooperatives, a cooperative bank (Laboral Kutxa), a university, R&D centers, and social services — each with its own institution and its own focus.

> **Intent:** Facebook's failure mode is consolidation — every social and economic verb absorbed into one product and optimized for attention. The platform's growth shape is the inverse: *outward spawning* into federated specialized infrastructure (Mondragon). Naming the anti-pattern explicitly is the way to keep "but we should add X" proposals legible as Facebook-shaped or not. When the proposed feature would absorb a verb that belongs in a future federated unit (banking, insurance, BI), the answer is "later, separately," not "yes, here."

This platform's job is to seed and sustain the conditions under which that federation can emerge. When a community fund reaches scale, banking is a separate platform. When enough cooperatives form, shared services is a separate platform. When the volume of local economic data is meaningful, intelligence is a separate platform. This platform stays focused on the foundational work: making collective action visible, repeatable, and durable in a place. The spawning is a feature, not a failure of consolidation.

## The loops, in order

The loops are organized into five families, each representing a deepening level of activation energy and stake. Members enter through the lightest loops and, over time, find themselves participating in deeper ones. No member is required to traverse the full sequence; the platform's job is to make each loop available and the next one slightly easier than it would be elsewhere.

---

### Family 1 — Gathering (the civic on-ramp)

The lightest, most universal loops. The pain point is shared and pre-political: people want to find their people, and the existing tools don't help. These loops are the platform's front door, because no belief is required to use them — only the desire to be less alone in a place.

#### Loop 1 — Find your people

A person discovers an existing recurring real-world gathering and wants to find it again. The Run Club that meets Thursdays at Drake's. The chess players at the park on Saturday mornings. The sewing circle at the bookstore Tuesday nights. The bird-watching group at the wetlands every other Sunday. Currently, the only way to find these groups is to physically be there and ask, then chase them across Strava, Instagram, and WhatsApp. The platform's surface: a public, locality-first, searchable index of recurring gatherings, with a clean URL the organizer can text to a friend, post to a group chat, or chalk on a board — and a search experience that lets a stranger filter by location, day, time, and kind to find what's nearby.

#### Loop 2 — Float an idea

A person has been thinking about starting something — a Sunday coffee walk, a monthly clothing swap, a beginner pickleball morning, a fermentation skill-share — but doesn't know if anyone would come, and doesn't want to commit to hosting before they know. The platform's surface: Wonder. *"I'm wondering if folks would be into..."* Others tap *"I'd be in."* Most Wonders fade quietly, and that is the design. The few that accumulate interest convert into recurring gatherings (Loop 4). The activation energy is the lowest possible: one sentence, no date, no place, no commitment.

#### Loop 3 — Land here

A person has just moved to a neighborhood and lost their network. They want to know what's happening within walking distance this week, that they could just show up to. The platform's surface: a no-login locality view organized around *"what's happening near me, with people I could meet."* Newcomers are the highest-intent users for this kind of platform — they have actively lost something and are looking for ways back in. This loop is also the most underserved by existing tools, which assume you already know who and what you're looking for.

#### Loop 4 — Gather regularly

An organizer who is already convening something — a Wonder that grew real, a longstanding running club, a repair café, a parents' meetup — wants to make their group findable, persistent, and easy to point newcomers to. The platform's surface: a public group page anchored to a place and a recurring time, with a single shareable URL the organizer can use instead of three apps. The pain point this solves directly: organizers currently doing the platform's work without the platform's tools.

---

### Family 2 — Sharing (mutual aid)

Once people are gathering, the natural next step is sharing what they have and asking for what they need. No money changes hands. These loops are where neighbors begin to recognize each other as resources and where the texture of community shifts from acquaintance to interdependence.

#### Loop 5 — Share what you have

A person has something to give, lend, or teach — extra zucchini from the garden, a pressure washer to borrow, a willingness to teach kombucha-making to anyone interested, two hours of yard help on Saturday. The platform's surface: an Offer post, identity-forward, anchored to a community and a place. The pain point: people who want to give don't know who needs what they have, and the friction of declaring publicly is high enough to suppress most generosity.

#### Loop 6 — Ask for help

A person needs something — a truck for a Saturday move, a babysitter recommendation, a stand mixer for a weekend, a hand fixing a leaky faucet. The platform's surface: an Ask post, identity-forward, with the same locality-first reach as Offers. The pain point: needing help and not knowing who to ask without imposing — and not wanting to surface the need in the wrong tool (anonymous neighborhood feeds curdle it, group chats are too narrow, Facebook is for strangers).

---

### Family 3 — Trade (the economic loop)

This is the substance of the platform — the loop that turns participation into local economic strength. Members who have come for gathering and stayed for mutual aid discover that the same neighbors are also makers, growers, and skilled tradespeople, and that buying close is the same gesture as showing up.

#### Loop 7 — Make and be found

A person who makes things — sourdough, candles, jam, knives, furniture — wants to be findable by neighbors who would buy from them. The platform's surface: a maker profile with QR onboarding at the booth, location-anchored, surfacing the maker across markets, off-days, and direct pickup arrangements. The pain point: making a real living from a craft requires being findable beyond the one market day, and the existing tools (Etsy, Instagram, Square) optimize for engagement, scale, or aggregation — never for the simple question, *"who near me makes this?"*

#### Loop 8 — Follow what you love

A person who has bought something good from a maker — at a market, from a Wonder-turned-Host, from a neighbor's Offer — wants to find them again, learn when they have new offerings, and return as a customer. The platform's surface: Follow, the standing form of a small public commitment to another person's work. Following is the retention mechanic of the economic loop. It is the only behavioral signal that matters early: *do people come back to the makers they discovered?* The answer determines whether the thesis works.

#### Loop 9 — Find a local pro

A person needs a plumber, a vet, an accountant, a mechanic, a hairdresser, an attorney, a piano teacher. The platform's surface: locality-first Service Provider profiles, with the Saved primitive (quiet, persistent, no notification stream) for building a personal local network of trusted contacts. The pain point: when a need arises, the existing tools (Yelp, Angi, HomeAdvisor) charge tradespeople for visibility, gate trust behind a star-rating system that hurts small operators, and surface aggregator-friendly results over neighbor-friendly ones. The community-anchored alternative — *the plumber your neighbor used and trusts* — has no clean digital surface.

> **Intent:** This is the wedge claim. The platform's value here is in surfacing a relationship pattern that already works offline — neighbor-to-neighbor recommendation. If the digital surface adds friction (review forms, ranking algorithms, notification streams) or breaks the trust pattern (anonymous reviews, paid placement), the feature failed even if engagement metrics look fine. Score every service-provider proposal against "would the trusted-neighbor pattern still operate under this?" before shipping.

---

### Family 4 — Pooling (community ownership)

The loops where members stop being participants and become stakeholders. Capital is pooled, businesses are founded, resources are shared, and the community begins to literally own things together. This is where the squeeze is most directly answered: ownership concentration is the squeeze; collective ownership is the antidote (the wealth-circulation-over-extraction absolute lives in [`../foundation/principles.md`](../foundation/principles.md)).

#### Loop 10 — Start something

A person has an idea for something that would grow the community's economic capacity — start a bakery, take over a closing coffee shop, form a cooperative daycare, launch a worker-owned cleaning service. The platform's surface: an Initiative (per `initiatives.md`). Encouragement signals interest, Pledge signals commitment, a linked Gathering brings the right people into a room. The pain point: people have ideas constantly, and the gap between *thinking about it* and *finding out if the community would back it* is the gap that kills most new local businesses before they exist.

#### Loop 11 — Pool resources

A specific kind of Initiative: members combine capital to acquire something a single household could not — a piece of land for a community garden and orchard, a wood shop's tools when the owner retires, the building that houses a beloved corner store when the landlord puts it on the market, a piece of agricultural land for a CSA. The platform's surface: structured pledges (capital, time, customers, mentorship) accumulating against a stated target, with handoff to partner CDFIs and securities-compliant capital structures for the actual financial transaction. The pain point: capital concentration is what makes ownership inaccessible to ordinary people; pooling is the structural answer, and it is currently nearly impossible to organize without lawyers, brokers, and existing networks.

#### Loop 12 — Steward what we built

A community now owns something — a garden, a tool library, a kitchen co-op, a building, a cooperative business — and that ownership requires ongoing care: monthly workdays, a Saturday open-hours rotation, a treasurer, a maintenance schedule, a way for new members to find their way in. The platform's surface: a Steward role and the long-tail surface for organizing the upkeep of shared things. The pain point: shared resources fail more often from atrophied stewardship than from any external pressure, and the existing tools for coordinating ongoing care (group texts, spreadsheets, Slack) decay quickly as memberships turn over.

---

### Family 5 — Federation (the spawn boundary)

#### Loop 13 — Federate and spawn

A community has grown to the point where its needs exceed what this platform should try to do. Its fund needs real banking infrastructure. Its cooperatives need shared bookkeeping, insurance, and legal templates. Its Initiatives generate data that should inform regional economic decisions. The platform's surface here is **the handoff** — the federation interface to dedicated, separate platforms purpose-built for each domain. A community development financial institution. A cooperative services federation. A regional intelligence platform. Each is its own thing, with its own focus, its own governance, and its own technology — connected to this platform through federation protocols and shared identity, not absorbed into it.

This loop is the platform's most important architectural commitment. It is the answer to the question *what stops this from becoming Facebook.* The answer: deliberate spawning at the moment when depth would otherwise force the platform to dilute. A platform trying to do banking, insurance, business intelligence, gathering coordination, maker discovery, and Initiative organizing all at once will fail at all of them. A platform that does the foundational coordination work well, and hands off to dedicated federated infrastructure as communities mature, can stay focused indefinitely.

## Why this order

The order is not arbitrary, and it is not stylistic. It reflects three concurrent gradients:

> **Intent:** The order isn't a roadmap; it's a *constraint on which loops can be shipped before which*. A feature in Family 4 (Pool) that doesn't lean on Loops 1–9's accumulated participation is asking strangers for capital, which fails. Use the order to spot loop-skipping proposals — when a scenario advances a deeper-loop behavior without the upstream substrate present (or earning its way), the gradients below are the reason to push back.

**Activation energy ascends.** Loop 1 (find your people) requires nothing of the user — they open the app, search a locality, and land on the page for a gathering they didn't previously know existed. Loop 13 (federate and spawn) requires a community that has already organized capital, founded businesses, and reached the threshold where dedicated infrastructure is justified. Each loop in the sequence asks slightly more of its participants than the one above it. Members enter at whatever loop matches their current readiness and move down the sequence, or not, at their own pace.

**Belief required ascends.** Loop 1 requires no belief about the economy — wanting to find the running club is pre-political. Loop 7 begins to lean on the local-multiplier thesis. Loop 11 requires participants to believe that pooled ownership is real. Loop 13 requires a worked-out conviction about cooperative federation as a structural alternative to corporate consolidation. The sequence lets people experience the thesis before they have to believe it. Most participants who eventually act on the deeper claims got there by participating in the lighter loops first.

**Stake accumulates.** Every loop produces an event in the Member's record — a join, an interest, an offer fulfilled, a follow, a pledge, a stewardship rotation. By the time a Member is participating in Family 4, they have accumulated months or years of participation in Families 1–3, and their participation in deeper loops is anchored in real, visible, durable history rather than abstract willingness. The platform's most consequential long-term surface — the stakeholder dashboard at Member T3 — is the readout of that accumulation. Stake is not asserted; it is earned, loop by loop, over time.

The order also matters because **each loop leans on the ones above it.** Initiatives (Loop 10) work because the community already knows each other through gathering (Family 1), sharing (Family 2), and trade (Family 3). Skip the upper loops, and Initiatives become abstract calls for capital from strangers. Skip Family 1, and the platform is a directory; skip Family 2, and trade is transactional rather than relational; skip Family 3, and pooling has no economic substrate. The cumulative architecture is the design.

## The Mondragon trajectory

Mondragon began in 1956 with a single small cooperative — five workers and a furnace, in a Basque town that had been hollowed out by industrial decline. Seventy years later, it is a federation of ninety-five cooperatives, eighty thousand worker-owners, eleven billion euros in revenue, with its own bank, university, and R&D centers. It did not get there by becoming a single platform that did everything. It got there by staying disciplined about what each institution was for, federating across them, and letting depth emerge through specialization rather than consolidation.

This platform is the seed institution. Its job is to make collective action in a place visible, repeatable, and durable enough that the deeper institutions — the bank, the cooperative services federation, the intelligence layer, the education arm — have something to grow out of. It does not need to be all of those things. It needs to be the place where ordinary people in a community find each other, show up for each other, and gradually build the conditions under which the deeper institutions become inevitable rather than aspirational.

The loops in this document are how that seeding happens. Each one is small. Together, in order, over time, they are how a community grows into a Mondragon.
