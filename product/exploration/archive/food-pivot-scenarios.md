# Main Street Market — Founding Scenarios (Food Pivot)

## Product Context

Main Street Market is pivoting from a general local-business map to a **food-first platform** that connects consumers with local farmers, ranchers, and their food. The product helps people understand where their food comes from, who grows it, and why it matters — then makes it easy to buy directly from those producers.

The platform replaces the DIY (Do It Yourself) mindset with **DIT — Do It Together** — fostering community-based action around food: group buys, shared CSA pickups, neighborhood food co-ops, and collective support of local agriculture.

### North Stars
1. Connect people to the farmers and ranchers who feed them
2. Make local food accessible, understandable, and social — not precious or preachy

### Strategic Framing
- **Pro-competition, pro-free-market language** — not progressive activism branding
- Food is the wedge; the platform can expand to other local businesses later
- Content creators who cover food supply issues are natural distribution partners
- TikTok/Instagram content from farmers embeds directly into the platform — we don't rebuild what already works
- Food brand transparency (who owns the brand, how it's processed, where the money goes) is a key differentiator from apps like Yuka that only score health

## Scenario 1 — The Consumer

**Title:** Sarah finds her food and the people who grow it

**Narrative:**

Sarah is a working mom in a mid-sized city. She's been seeing TikTok videos about ultra-processed food, how most grocery store brands are owned by the same few corporations, and how the food supply chain prioritizes shelf life over nutrition. She's alarmed but overwhelmed. She doesn't know what to actually *do* about it. She doesn't have time to research every brand, drive to three different stores, or become a homesteader.

A friend shares a link from Main Street Market. Sarah opens it and sees a map of farms and ranches near her — closer than she expected. She taps on one: "Meadow Creek Farm. Family-owned since 1987. 45 minutes from you. Pasture-raised eggs, seasonal vegetables, grass-fed beef." There's a short TikTok video the farmer posted showing the morning egg collection. It feels real. Below the video is a simple breakdown: what's available this week, pickup locations, and pricing.

Sarah notices a section called "Know Your Brands" — she scans a barcode on her pantry pasta sauce. The app shows her: the brand is owned by a multinational conglomerate, the tomatoes are sourced from industrial farms in three countries, and the sauce contains seven additives. Right below that, the app suggests a local alternative: a small-batch sauce made by a family operation 60 miles away, available through a nearby pickup point. The ingredients are tomatoes, olive oil, garlic, basil, salt.

Sarah isn't ready to overhaul her entire grocery list. But she orders a dozen eggs and a jar of that sauce for pickup on Saturday. The pickup point is at a church parking lot ten minutes from her house where three other farms also drop off orders. She meets another mom picking up a farm box. They talk. The other mom mentions a DIT group buy happening next month — a neighborhood is going in together on a quarter cow from a local ranch, splitting the cost and the cuts. Sarah joins the group.

Over the next few weeks, Sarah checks the app before her regular grocery run. She's not replacing everything — she's replacing a few things at a time, and each swap feels like a small win. She watches a creator video embedded on the app that explains why commodity eggs cost $2.99 and farm eggs cost $6 — not to shame her, but to show her where each dollar goes. She starts to understand the difference between price and value.

She shares the app with her sister.

**What success looks like:**

Sarah went from anxious and overwhelmed to making one small change — without guilt, without preaching, without needing a nutrition degree. The app met her where she was, showed her what was nearby, made the first step easy, and connected her to other people doing the same thing. She didn't have to become someone different. She just had to see what was already there.

### Capabilities surfaced

| Capability | What it enables |
|---|---|
| Farm/ranch discovery map | Consumer finds nearby producers on a map with distance, products, and story |
| Farm profile with embedded video | Farmer tells their own story via TikTok/Instagram embeds — no content creation by the platform |
| Product availability | Current week's offerings, pricing, and pickup/delivery options displayed per farm |
| Brand transparency scanner | Consumer scans a grocery product barcode, sees ownership, sourcing, and ingredients in plain language |
| Local alternative suggestions | When a scanned brand is corporate/industrial, the app surfaces a local producer alternative |
| Pickup point network | Shared community locations where multiple farms drop off orders — reduces logistics for both sides |
| DIT group buy | Neighbors organize collective purchases (e.g., split a quarter cow) through the platform |
| Creator content integration | Videos from food/agriculture content creators embed on relevant product and farm pages |
| Progressive engagement | App doesn't demand an overhaul — it supports incremental swaps and celebrates small wins |
| Social sharing | Simple share flow to spread a farm, product, or the app itself to friends and family |

### Success criteria (pass/fail)

- **PASS:** Consumer can open the app, see farms near them on a map, and tap one to see what it offers — in under 60 seconds
- **PASS:** Consumer can scan a grocery product barcode and see a plain-language summary of ownership and sourcing
- **PASS:** Consumer can place an order from a farm and select a pickup point — without creating an account first (account created at checkout)
- **PASS:** Consumer can view embedded TikTok/Instagram content on a farm profile without leaving the app
- **PASS:** Consumer can join a DIT group buy from another user's shared link
- **PASS:** Consumer can browse and swap one item at a time — the app never presents an all-or-nothing proposition
- **FAIL:** App requires consumer to state dietary preferences, political beliefs, or income level before showing results
- **FAIL:** Brand scanner shames the consumer for their current choices (language must be neutral and informational)
- **FAIL:** Farm discovery requires the consumer to know what they're looking for in advance (browsing must be the default)

## Scenario 2 — The Farmer / Rancher

**Title:** Jim gets found by the people who want what he grows

**Narrative:**

Jim runs a 200-acre cattle and vegetable operation outside a growing metro area. He's third generation on this land. He sells grass-fed beef and seasonal produce. His customers love him — the problem is he doesn't have enough of them. He does a farmers market on Saturdays, runs a small CSA with 30 members, and sells some beef by the quarter and half to families who find him through word of mouth. He's on Instagram but posts inconsistently. He made a TikTok once showing a calf being born and it got 40,000 views, but he has no idea how to turn that into sales.

Jim's wife hears about Main Street Market from a farming Facebook group. Jim is skeptical — he's been burned by platforms that promise exposure and deliver nothing, or that take a cut he can't afford. He looks at the sign-up flow: name, location, what he produces, a short bio in his own words, and an optional link to his TikTok or Instagram. No subscription fee to list. It takes him eight minutes on his phone, sitting in his truck after the morning feed.

His profile goes live. It shows his farm on the map with a pin. He writes: "Third-generation ranch. Grass-fed beef, no hormones, no feedlot. Seasonal vegetables grown in the same dirt my grandfather planted in." The app pulls in his TikTok video of the calf — it shows up right on his profile. He adds his current availability: quarter beef boxes, eggs by the dozen, and a summer vegetable box starting in June.

He sets up two pickup options: on-farm pickup on Fridays, and a shared drop point at a feed store in town that already lets three other farms use their parking lot on Wednesdays. He sets his own prices. The platform doesn't negotiate, doesn't suggest, doesn't take a percentage of the sale — it charges a small flat monthly fee after a free trial period.

Within two weeks, Jim gets four new customers he's never met. Two are from a suburb 30 miles away — they'd been looking for local beef and didn't know he existed. One found him by scanning a grocery store steak package and seeing "feedlot, corporate processor, shipped 1,200 miles" — then tapping the "find local alternative" button. The fourth customer is organizing a DIT group buy in her neighborhood and wants to order a whole beef, split eight ways. Jim's never sold a whole beef to a single group before. It's his most profitable single transaction of the year.

Jim starts posting to TikTok more — a 30-second video of moving cattle to fresh pasture, a time-lapse of tomatoes growing. Every video auto-pulls into his Main Street Market profile. He doesn't have to cross-post or manage another platform. He notices that the app shows him simple stats: how many people viewed his profile, what products they clicked on most, and which zip codes his viewers came from. He didn't ask for analytics but it helps him decide where to add another pickup point.

A content creator who covers regenerative agriculture reaches out through the platform to feature Jim's ranch in a video. Jim agrees. The creator's video embeds on Jim's profile alongside his own content. Jim gains twelve new customers that month.

At the end of the season, Jim has 55 CSA members (up from 30), regular beef customers filling his freezer orders three months out, and a waitlist for his eggs. He didn't build a website, hire a marketer, or learn a new platform. He just showed up as himself, and the platform put him in front of people who were already looking for exactly what he does.

**What success looks like:**

Jim got discovered by customers who were actively seeking what he produces — without paying for ads, learning marketing, or changing how he farms. The platform worked around his life, not the other way around. His content (posted wherever he already posts) became his storefront. And he maintained full control of his pricing, his inventory, and his customer relationships.

### Capabilities surfaced

| Capability | What it enables |
|---|---|
| Farm self-registration | Farmer creates a profile from their phone in under 10 minutes — name, location, products, bio, optional social links |
| Zero-cost listing (free trial) | No financial barrier to entry; farmer can evaluate the platform before committing |
| Flat-fee pricing model | Farmer pays a predictable monthly fee, not a percentage of sales — keeps margins intact |
| TikTok/Instagram auto-pull | Social content from the farmer's existing accounts automatically surfaces on their profile |
| Product and availability management | Farmer sets what's available this week, pricing, and quantities — updates from their phone |
| Pickup point configuration | Farmer defines on-farm pickup, shared drop points, or delivery areas with schedules |
| Farmer-controlled pricing | Platform never sets, suggests, or negotiates prices — the farmer decides |
| DIT group buy (farmer side) | Farmer receives and fulfills group orders from community-organized bulk purchases |
| Basic profile analytics | Simple view count, product interest, and geographic reach data — no marketing jargon |
| Content creator connection | Creators can discover and reach out to farmers through the platform for features and collaborations |
| Customer relationship ownership | Farmer sees and communicates with their customers directly — the platform doesn't disintermediate |

### Success criteria (pass/fail)

- **PASS:** Farmer can create a complete, live profile from a mobile phone in under 10 minutes
- **PASS:** Farmer's existing TikTok/Instagram content auto-displays on their profile without manual re-upload
- **PASS:** Farmer can update product availability and pricing in under 2 minutes
- **PASS:** Farmer can set up at least two pickup/delivery options with independent schedules
- **PASS:** Farmer can receive and view a DIT group buy order as a single consolidated order
- **PASS:** Farmer can see basic analytics (views, clicks, zip codes) without navigating a dashboard
- **PASS:** Farmer retains all customer contact information and is never locked out of their own customer list
- **PASS:** Platform charges a flat fee — never a percentage of the transaction
- **FAIL:** Registration requires a desktop computer, a business license, or any documentation beyond self-attestation
- **FAIL:** Platform inserts itself between the farmer and customer communication (e.g., anonymized messaging)
- **FAIL:** Platform suggests pricing, applies surge pricing, or discounts without farmer consent
- **FAIL:** Farmer's TikTok content is re-hosted or re-branded by the platform

### Language and framing guidelines

- Use **"Do It Together" (DIT)** not "community supported agriculture" or "co-op" — DIT is action-oriented and avoids loaded terminology
- Use **"know your food"** not "clean eating" or "organic lifestyle" — inclusive, not aspirational
- Use **"local alternative"** not "better option" — neutral comparison, not moral judgment
- Use **"where your dollar goes"** not "corporate greed" — pro-market framing, not anti-business
- Brand scanner results should be **informational, not editorial** — "owned by [parent company], sourced from [locations], contains [ingredients]" not "this is bad for you"
- Farm profiles should feel like **meeting a neighbor** not reading a certification — stories over credentials
- Never frame local food as a sacrifice or luxury — frame it as **a choice that was always available, just hard to find**
