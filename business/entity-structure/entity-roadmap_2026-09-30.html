Entity Roadmap

As of 2026-09-30 · Solo founder today → Operating Model v2 over time. Detail heavy at the start, open-ended at the end.

The core idea: don't build the elaborate cooperative structure before there's anyone in it. Start with the cheapest, most-convertible thing (a California LLC) and layer in complexity only when real people, real revenue, or real capital make the added structure worth its cost. Each phase's trigger is "this new thing exists," not "this much time has passed." Skip or delay a phase if its trigger hasn't fired.

Phases

Phase 0
Solo Founder Setup
Now (Sept–Oct 2026)
Trigger: You're deciding to build this.

Actions this month

  • File California LLC (Articles of Organization, $70 online)
  • Get EIN from IRS (free, online, instant)
  • Draft operating agreement with Statement of Intent as an exhibit
  • Open business bank account and business credit card
  • Transfer domain names into the LLC
  • File Statement of Information within 90 days ($20)
  • Set up basic bookkeeping (Wave free, or paid option)

Decisions to make

  • LLC name (can differ from product name — DBA for the product name)
  • Whether to add "Benefit LLC" designation now vs. plain LLC
  • First draft of the Statement of Intent language
Cost: ~$1,000–1,500 first year (state fees + tax + accounting setup + optional legal template)
Phase 1
Build & Validate
0–6 months
Trigger: You're building the product and starting to test with real users in one community.

Actions

  • Ship MVP under the LLC
  • File trademark applications for name and logo (~$350/class through USPTO)
  • Publish the Statement of Intent on the public site
  • Test in one geographic community (probably Sacramento area)
  • Track early metrics: usage, retention, willingness to pay
  • Keep receipts, contracts, and decision records — dated paper trail

Decisions to make

  • Which geographic community for first real users
  • What the initial community metrics are that you'll eventually put in the charter
  • Whether MVP validates the concept or needs pivoting
Cost: mostly your time, plus ~$500–1,500 in trademark filings and misc.
Phase 2
First Users & First Revenue
6–12 months
Trigger: Real users using it. First business listings paid. First member-to-member transaction runs through the platform.

Actions

  • Register for California seller's permit and any sales tax obligations
  • Understand money transmitter rules if you're touching transaction funds (this is a real legal issue — may need to route commerce through Stripe Connect or similar to avoid needing a license)
  • Start relationship-building calls with 2–3 attorneys — one startup/PBC-experienced, one nonprofit-experienced. No paid work yet.
  • Draft the future PBC charter language in parallel with real usage data
  • Consider S-corp election on the LLC if revenue is meaningful (tax benefit)
  • Formalize the initial community metrics you're going to hold the future board accountable to

Decision point

  • Is this going to be a real business? If yes, plan the Phase 3 conversion. If no, unwind the LLC cheaply.
Cost: ~$500–2,000 in permits, tax setup, exploratory legal conversations
Phase 3
Convert to PBC & First Employees
12–18 months (trigger-driven)
Trigger: You're about to hire someone, take capital, or the revenue is real enough that the LLC-founder structure no longer fits.

Actions

  • Form a Delaware or California PBC (choice depends on capital plans)
  • Convert or roll the LLC into the PBC (asset transfer or entity conversion)
  • Draft charter with named public benefit AND named community metrics AND dual-mandate board language
  • File for B Corp certification (real work, takes 6–12 months of its own)
  • Establish initial board (you + 2 trusted advisors is enough to start)
  • Design employee equity: non-voting economic interests structure
  • First real legal spend on entity formation

Decisions to make

  • Delaware vs. California PBC
  • Dual-mandate tie-breaker language (community-primary, co-equal, or rebuttable presumption)
  • Employee equity vehicle specifics
  • Charter metrics — final wording
Cost: ~$10,000–25,000 in legal + $500–1,000 filing fees + ongoing B Corp cert fees
Phase 4
Member Cooperative Layer
18–24+ months (trigger-driven)
Trigger: There's a real member and business community. Enough that member governance is meaningful, not theater.

Actions (higher-level; refine when the trigger fires)

  • Form the member cooperative (state form depends on final legal structure chosen)
  • Or form the (c)(6) association if going the trade-association path
  • Transfer voting control from you to the cooperative
  • Set up election mechanisms for member representatives
  • Start the flow-through mechanism from PBC to community grant funds
Cost: ~$15,000–40,000 depending on structure complexity
Phase 5
Mission Lock Vehicle
When it matters (2+ years, or earlier if capital enters)
Trigger: Taking outside capital that could threaten mission, OR reaching a scale where founder-death or founder-exit risk becomes a real concern.

Actions (sketch; nail down when the trigger fires)

  • Form Purpose Trust or Long-Term Benefit Trust
  • Transfer golden-share or veto-share equivalent to the trust
  • This is the Patagonia / Anthropic move — powerful but expensive and irreversible
Cost: ~$50,000+ specialized legal, takes months
Phase 6
Federation
When a second community wants in
Trigger: Another community, city, or organization wants to run its own instance of the platform.

Actions (open-ended; adapt when it happens)

  • Publish federation protocol
  • Define licensing terms
  • Set up cross-community governance layer
  • Playbook-sharing infrastructure
Cost: variable; likely revenue-positive from that point forward
Phase 7+
Adapt
Whenever
Trigger: The world changes. The model changes with it.

By this phase, real evidence has replaced planning assumptions. The specifics of what comes next should be decided against actual usage, actual community feedback, actual regulatory environment — not against decisions made years earlier when everything was theory. Deliberately open.

Protecting the platform in the meantime

Elaborate structure isn't the only way to protect mission intent. Cheap moves at Phase 0 create a paper trail that's just as durable, and matters if anyone (a future co-founder, an investor, a court) ever challenges what this platform was always meant to be.

  • Statement of Intent in the LLC operating agreement AND published on the site AND referenced in every early contract. Dated. Signed.
  • Trademark on name and logo — protects against a bad actor using your identity later.
  • All IP owned by the LLC, not you personally. Assign the code, the domain names, the trademarks.
  • Public roadmap — publishing this document (or a version of it) creates a public commitment. Reputation cost of walking it back is real.
  • Dated decision log — every material decision, dated, in a file you keep with the entity records. Future evidence of continuous intent.
  • Founder pre-commitment letter to yourself — a signed statement acknowledging that you will not sell, pivot to extractive model, or take capital that requires it. Not legally binding on its own but powerful evidence of original intent.

Not legal advice. California-specific details (LLC fees, franchise tax, permit requirements) reflect working knowledge that should be verified against current state filings and against your specific situation with an attorney or accountant. Cost ranges are indicative, not quotes.
